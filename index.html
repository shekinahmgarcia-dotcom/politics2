MODULE II.Politics

Politics is not a spectator sport, and politicians are not celebrities to worship. This module is about power — where it comes from, who it belongs to, and how to use yours. No sides. Just your rights, your vote, and your voice.

01 — The Big Idea

Politics is everyone’s business

You don't have to run for office to be political. Every law, every classroom budget, every jeepney fare — politics touches all of it.

Politics is the process of deciding “who gets what, when, and how” in a society — how power is gained, shared, used, and questioned. Political science studies these processes; citizenship means taking part in them.

— after Harold Lasswell · UCSP, DepEd SHS Curriculum

⚖ Power & Authority

Power is the ability to influence others. Authority is power that people recognize as legitimate — and in a democracy, legitimacy comes from the people.

🏛 Governance

How society is steered: making rules, delivering services, and being held accountable — at barangay, local, and national levels.

”
Sovereignty resides in the people, and all government authority emanates from them.
ARTICLE II, SECTION 1 · 1987 CONSTITUTION OF THE PHILIPPINES

Read that again. The government doesn't grant you power — you lend power to the government. Every official, from barangay captain to president, holds borrowed authority. And what is borrowed can be held to account.

02 — Know Your Rights

The Bill of Rights is yours

Rights you don't know about are rights you can't defend. These highlights come from Article III of the 1987 Constitution — written to protect you from the abuse of power.

ART. III · SEC. 1

Due Process & Equal Protection

No one shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law — and the law must protect everyone equally.

ART. III · SEC. 4

Speech, Expression & Press

You may speak, write, assemble, and petition the government. Criticizing officials is a right, not a crime of disloyalty.

ART. III · SEC. 5

Freedom of Religion

The free exercise of religion and belief is protected, and no religious test is required to hold public office.

ART. III · SEC. 3

Privacy

Your private communications and your person are protected against unreasonable searches and seizures.

ART. III · SEC. 7

Right to Information

You have the right to information on matters of public concern — official records, documents, and government transactions.

ART. III · SEC. 8

Freedom of Association

You may form unions, organizations, or movements — including ones that criticize the government.

Rights come with responsibilities. Exercise your freedoms without violating the rights of others — and be ready to defend these rights not just for yourself, but for people you disagree with. That's how rights survive.
03 — They Serve You

Public servants, not idols

Admire good work — but never surrender your judgment to any politician. Personality cults are how accountability dies.

“Public office is a public trust. Public officers and employees must at all times be accountable to the people, serve them with utmost responsibility, integrity, loyalty, and efficiency… and lead modest lives.”

ARTICLE XI, SECTION 1 · 1987 CONSTITUTION

Healthy citizenship ✦

  • Support platforms and principles, not surnames or slogans
  • Praise good policies — and still question them
  • Judge officials by track record and evidence
  • Change your mind when the facts change
  • Remember: officials are employees of the people

Warning signs of idolatry ✕

  • Defending a politician no matter what they do
  • Attacking anyone who criticizes "your" candidate
  • Believing one person alone can save the country
  • Excusing corruption because "they all do it"
  • Loyalty to a person over loyalty to the Constitution
04 — The Enemy Is Corruption, Not Each Other

Corruption: how it steals from you

Corruption isn't an abstract scandal — it's a classroom that was never built, medicine that never arrived, a road that crumbles in a year.

What it looks like

Common forms

  • Grift & bribery — public funds or favors exchanged for personal gain
  • Nepotism & cronyism — positions for relatives and friends, not the qualified
  • Kickbacks — inflated contracts with a cut returned to officials
  • Vote-buying — trading cash or goods for your ballot
What it costs

The real price

  • Fewer classrooms, hospitals, and services — you pay twice: taxes lost, services denied
  • Public trust erodes; cynicism replaces participation
  • The poor pay the highest price — petty bribes hit hardest at the bottom
  • Honest workers and businesses are pushed out

What citizens can do — starting now

  • Use your right to information. Ask how funds are spent — budgets and projects are public documents.
  • Refuse to sell your vote. Vote-buying money lasts a day; bad governance lasts years.
  • Report through proper channels — the Ombudsman, COA, and Civil Service Commission exist to receive complaints.
  • Support transparency. Follow how your barangay, city, and agencies spend — many publish budgets online.
  • Vote and volunteer. Corruption thrives in low turnout and silence.
05 — Your Voting Power

One vote. Real power.

Elections are the one day a billionaire and a student have exactly the same power. Here's how to make yours count.

Register

Register with COMELEC as soon as you're eligible. No registration, no vote — mark deadlines early and keep your records updated.

Research

Study candidates' track records, platforms, and sources of funding — not their jingles, posters, or viral clips.

Scrutinize

Attend forums, read multiple sources, and ask: what have they actually done? Who benefits from their policies?

Vote & Guard

Cast your vote freely, refuse bribes, and help protect the count — volunteer as a poll watcher if you can.

Your vote is not for sale. It is for the future.

Democracy isn't a gift from politicians — it's a system the people maintain. Voting is the beginning of citizenship, not the end: attend consultations, join your community, and keep watch between elections.

06 — Think Independently

Check your bias at the door

Being non-partisan doesn't mean having no opinions. It means your opinions are earned through evidence — not inherited from family, barkada, or a feed algorithm.

BIAS CHECK 01

Bandwagon effect

"Everyone supports them, so I should too." Crowds are not evidence. Decide based on track record and platform, not popularity.

BIAS CHECK 02

Halo effect

Charming, good-looking, or funny ≠ competent. Evaluate the whole record, including the parts that aren't photogenic.

BIAS CHECK 03

Confirmation bias

We seek news that agrees with us and skip what doesn't. Deliberately read sources that challenge your view before deciding.

BIAS CHECK 04

Us vs. them

Politics is not a basketball rivalry. People who vote differently are fellow citizens with shared problems — not enemies to defeat.

The Citizen's Stance

I serve no politician. They serve the people — including me.

I will know my rights and defend them — even for people I disagree with. I will support principles, not personalities. I will refuse vote-buying, question every official respectfully but firmly, and remember that in a democracy, the people are not the audience. We are the authority.

Power to the people — always.
